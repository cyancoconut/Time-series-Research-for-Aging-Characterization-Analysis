\title{Signal-based decomposition of battery check-up tests without a
step-by-step protocol description: chemistry-portable identification of
capacity, pulse and quasi-OCV segments across NMC, LFP and sodium-ion cells}

\author[isea]{\todo{author list}}
\address[isea]{\todo{affiliation}}

\begin{abstract}
Reference performance tests (RPTs, or check-ups) are the measurements that make
an ageing dataset interpretable: they contain the capacity test, the resistance
pulses and the quasi-open-circuit-voltage sweep from which state of health,
internal resistance and the OCV curve are derived. Yet the software that
processes public ageing datasets routinely discards them, because locating them
requires knowing the test protocol. Existing tools resolve this by trusting
metadata: either the cycler's own cycle index, or an experimenter-authored
step-index map that assigns a meaning to every step of every protocol. Both tie
the analysis to one laboratory's bookkeeping.
%
We present a method that locates and decomposes check-up tests from the
measured signal, requiring only a small set of physical cell and test
parameters --- voltage limits, nominal capacity and the target C-rates --- and
neither a step-by-step protocol description nor per-source code. The method
segments a raw time series into procedures, describes each segment with
scale-normalised features, and assigns capacity (CAP), pulse (PUL) and
quasi-OCV (qOCV) labels.
%
We report four experiments. First, an ablation over the segmentation stage shows
that the protocol columns it currently reads are not load-bearing: the cycler's
state column is redundant with the sign of the measured current to
\SI{0.0008}{\percent} of \num{131442261} samples, and boundary placement driven
by current alone recovers \SI{100}{\percent} of check-up segments intact --- no
segment merged or lost --- on nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC), lithium iron
phosphate (LFP) and sodium-ion (SIB) cells. Second, a leave-one-chemistry-out
transfer test shows that a classifier trained on NMC alone recovers every
capacity test on unseen LFP and sodium-ion cells given only their physical
parameters, provided its label space is scale-free; a classifier trained on
absolute C-rates fails completely on LFP.
%
Third, on \num{210} check-ups the method detects every one that is not a
fragmentary or aborted file, with no false detections on any chemistry, while a
fixed-current detector of the kind used to discard RPTs in existing pipelines
matches it on the chemistry it was calibrated for and finds nothing at all on
either unseen one. Under controlled folds the difference is attributable to the
label space rather than to the features: held-out capacity recall is
\SI{0}{\percent} when the capacity C-rate must be learned and \SI{100}{\percent}
when it is supplied as configuration, for every feature set tried. Fourth, on
\num{177} reference tests from two independent laboratories, given only their
published cell parameters and no target-laboratory labels, the parameterised
route recovers every capacity test. That experiment also exposed a latent defect
in the unsupervised route --- a cluster-selection rule our own protocols can
never reach, which cost \SI{90}{\percent} of one laboratory's capacity tests
until corrected --- illustrating that cross-laboratory validation tests the
implementation as much as the generalisation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Battery ageing studies produce two kinds of data. The bulk of it is cycling:
long, repetitive load profiles that age the cell. Interleaved with it, at
intervals of days to months, sits the \emph{check-up} or reference performance
test (RPT) --- a short, carefully specified sequence that measures the cell
rather than ageing it. A typical check-up contains a full capacity test at a
defined C-rate, a series of current pulses for resistance, and a low-rate
quasi-OCV sweep. Almost every quantity a battery paper reports about degradation
--- state of health, resistance growth, the shift of the OCV curve, differential
voltage features --- is extracted from those few hours, not from the cycling
around them.

Locating the check-up in a raw cycler export is therefore the precondition for
almost any downstream analysis, and it is exactly the step that current tooling
externalises to metadata. The recently published BatteryLife benchmark
\cite{batterylife} unifies sixteen ageing datasets, and does so with one
preprocessing script per source; the check-ups are not analysed but
\emph{removed}, in one case by a hard-coded current threshold, in another by
parsing only the files whose names mark them as cycling. PyProBE
\cite{pyprobe}, published in a software venue, takes the opposite and more
principled route --- it models the experiment explicitly --- but requires the
experimenter to author a step-index map (``steps 9--12 = discharge pulses'')
for every protocol, including manual reconciliation of cycler quirks.

These are not competing implementations of the same idea; they occupy different
points on a single axis, namely \emph{how much protocol metadata is needed to
find structure in the signal}. BatteryLife trusts the cycler's cycle index.
PyProBE trusts a human-authored step index. Both are reasonable engineering
choices, and both mean the analysis does not transfer to a source that indexes
its steps differently, or at all.

This paper asks whether the structure can be recovered from the measurement
itself. Our contribution is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A method that segments a raw check-up time series and labels its capacity,
pulse and quasi-OCV sections using only the measured current, voltage and time,
plus a handful of physical scalars that are printed on any cell datasheet or
fixed by the test plan (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A measurement of how much of that method's boundary detection actually
depends on protocol metadata, by ablating the metadata columns and quantifying
what breaks (Section~\ref{sec:res-metadata}). This is a claim that is easy to
assert and rarely tested; we test it.
\item A leave-one-chemistry-out transfer study over NMC, LFP and sodium-ion
cells (Section~\ref{sec:res-loco}), including a negative result --- a
conventionally-trained classifier that fails on an unseen chemistry --- and the
design property that fixes it.
\item A controlled separation of \emph{which} design property carries that
transfer (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation}), and a cross-laboratory test on two
independent external sources (Section~\ref{sec:res-external}) in which the
same property decides between recovering all \num{177} of their reference
tests and recovering one in ten.
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit about what is \emph{not} claimed. The method is not
metadata-free: it requires physical cell and test parameters, listed in
Section~\ref{sec:inputs}. The distinction we defend is between roughly five
known scalars and a full procedural description of the experiment; we argue in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} that this distinction is the one that governs
portability.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem statement and inputs}
\label{sec:inputs}

The input is a raw cycler export of one cell: a time series of current $I(t)$,
terminal voltage $V(t)$ and, where available, surface temperature $T(t)$,
covering an arbitrary mixture of ageing and check-up activity. The output is a
partition of that series into segments, each carrying one of the labels CAP,
PUL, qOCV or ``other''.

Besides the signal, the method requires the parameters in
Table~\ref{tab:inputs}. These are physical properties of the cell and targets of
the test plan. They are known before any measurement is made, they are the same
for every cell of a given type, and none of them describes the \emph{order or
composition} of the protocol.

\subsection{Segmentation}
\label{sec:segmentation}

Segmentation partitions the series into procedures. A boundary is placed where
the cell's electrical \emph{state} changes --- rest, charge or discharge --- and
where a rest exceeds $\tau_{\mathrm{pau}}$. The state is read from the current,
\begin{equation}
s(t) =
\begin{cases}
\text{rest} & |I(t)| < \varepsilon\,Q_{\mathrm{nom}} \\
\text{charge} & I(t) \ge \varepsilon\,Q_{\mathrm{nom}} \\
\text{discharge} & I(t) \le -\varepsilon\,Q_{\mathrm{nom}},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon$ a dimensionless zero-current tolerance.
Section~\ref{sec:res-metadata} shows the result is insensitive to $\varepsilon$
over two and a half orders of magnitude, so it is not a tuned threshold.

Segments shorter than $n_{\min}$ samples are routed to a discard bucket, as are
the interiors of long rests, which are reduced to their first and last sample so
that relaxation voltage and rest duration remain available downstream without
carrying the intervening samples.

\subsection{Segment features}
\label{sec:features}

Each segment is reduced to a feature vector chosen to be invariant to cell size
and, as far as possible, to chemistry. Currents are divided by
$Q_{\mathrm{nom}}$, making them C-rates; voltages are mapped onto
$[0,1]$ by $(V-V_{\min})/(V_{\max}-V_{\min})$. The vector comprises the current
profile ($I_{\text{mean}}$, $I_{\text{std}}$, $I_{\max}$, $I_{\min}$,
$I_{\text{range}}$, $|I|_{\text{mean}}$), the voltage \emph{edges}
($V_{\max}$, $V_{\min}$, $V_{\text{range}}$), duration
($\Delta t$ and $\log(1+\Delta t)$), and one context feature.

Two choices deserve comment. First, voltage \emph{shape} descriptors (mean and
standard deviation of $V$ within the segment) and temperature are deliberately
excluded as the most chemistry- and fixture-bound quantities available
\todo{this is a design rationale until the add-them-back ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} is run --- do not claim ``costs no accuracy''
until it is}. Second, a segment is not interpretable
in isolation --- a capacity discharge and a preparatory discharge differ mainly
in what preceded them --- so we add the normalised end-of-segment voltage of the
most recent non-rest predecessor. This is the signal a rule-based capacity test
keys on (a true capacity discharge follows a full charge), made available to a
learned model.

\subsection{Segment labelling}
\label{sec:labelling}

We evaluate two labelling routes over identical features.

\paragraph{Unsupervised.} Two-layer HDBSCAN followed by physically-motivated
masks: a capacity segment is a full discharge at $C_{\mathrm{cap}}$ preceded by
a full charge; a quasi-OCV segment is a full sweep at $C_{\mathrm{qocv}}$; a
pulse is a short excursion. The masks are expressed in the normalised feature
space, so they are stated once and evaluated per cell with that cell's
parameters.

\paragraph{Supervised.} A random forest over the same feature vector. The label
space matters more than the model: Section~\ref{sec:res-loco} shows that
training on absolute pipeline labels bakes in the training chemistry's C-rate
and does not transfer, whereas training on a \emph{scale-free} description of
segment shape (full or partial, charge or discharge, at a measured C-rate) and
resolving that description against the target cell's $C_{\mathrm{cap}}$ and
$C_{\mathrm{qocv}}$ at inference time does transfer.

\subsection{Downstream extraction}

Labelled segments feed the quantities a check-up exists to produce: capacity by
coulomb counting the CAP segment, pulse resistance from the PUL segments and
their adjacent rests, and the OCV curve from the qOCV branches. These are
standard and are not a contribution of this work; they are described only so far
as they are used to validate labels in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{How much does segmentation depend on protocol metadata?}
\label{sec:res-metadata}

The claim that structure is recovered from the signal is only as good as the
segmentation stage, which in our implementation reads two columns written by the
cycler: a procedure name and a state label. We therefore ablated them.

Three configurations were compared over the full fleet
(\num{10} cells, \num{131442261} samples, \num{13025} segments):
\textbf{L0}, the production segmenter, using rest-based boundaries, a
procedure-name change, and a state change inside a nominated procedure;
\textbf{L1}, L0 without the procedure-name rule; and \textbf{L2}, in which the
state label is replaced by Eq.~\eqref{eq:state} and the state-change rule is
applied everywhere --- so that \emph{no} protocol column is read. L2 is not
simply ``L1 with more removed'': it also promotes the state rule to a general
one. That is deliberate. The question is not how much a stripped-down segmenter
loses, but whether the signal carries the structure the protocol columns supply.

Because the ablation re-implements the segmenter, its L0 output is verified
against the production implementation sample by sample; a single differing
sample aborts the comparison. All ten cells passed.

\paragraph{The state column carries no information.}
Comparing the cycler's state label against Eq.~\eqref{eq:state}, \num{1100} of
\num{131442261} samples disagree (\SI{0.0008}{\percent}). Recall of the rest
class is exactly \num{1.000} on every cell; precision is at least
\num{0.99986}, and is flat for $\varepsilon$ between \num{e-4} and \num{3e-2}
--- a factor of \num{300}. The residual disagreement is not a chemistry effect
but a few hundred samples of cycler bookkeeping states recorded at zero current,
plus roughly \num{600} samples caught at a current zero crossing.

\paragraph{The procedure name fires often and decides little.}
Table~\ref{tab:rule3} shows that a majority of procedure-name boundaries occur
where no other rule fires --- which is why the dependence looked severe --- but
that \SIrange{81}{87}{\percent} of those cut inside a long rest or a
sub-threshold fragment that is discarded regardless. The naive count overstates
the dependence by roughly a factor of six.

The shape of the L1 loss is diagnostic. Every one of the \num{376} segments L1
merges is a pulse (\num{349} test pulses, \num{27} restore pulses); capacity and
quasi-OCV segments never break at any level. Inspecting the procedure-name
boundaries that L1 loses and L2 recovers shows why: they are rest-to-charge
transitions following a rest \emph{shorter} than $\tau_{\mathrm{pau}}$. No
rest-based rule can fire there, so L1 has nothing to cut with --- but the state
changes, and that change is present in the current. The procedure name was
supplying timing, not information.

That L2 outperforms L1 while reading strictly less metadata is the central
result of this section: the boundaries are in the signal, and the protocol
columns were a convenience.

\paragraph{What L2 does cost.} Outside the check-up taxonomy, L2 disturbs the
rest structure substantially (on NMC, \num{1161} merged and \num{302} lost
non-check-up segments). Rest stubs supply the predecessor-voltage context
feature of Section~\ref{sec:features} and the relaxation window used for pulse
resistance, so a segmenter built on L2 would need to treat rests explicitly.
Separately, impedance-spectroscopy dwell windows are indistinguishable from
rests by current alone. Neither affects the CAP/PUL/qOCV result, and both are
stated here rather than deferred to the limitations section because they bound
what the result licenses.

\subsection{Cross-chemistry transfer}
\label{sec:res-loco}

We next ask whether the \emph{labelling} stage transfers to a chemistry it has
never seen, given only that chemistry's physical parameters and no labelled
examples from it.

The unsupervised route transfers directly. Applied to LFP with only
Table~\ref{tab:inputs} changed, it recovers all \num{16} check-ups, yielding a
monotonic capacity trajectory from \SI{98.3}{\percent} to \SI{60.2}{\percent} of
nominal, with a first measured capacity of \SI{1.18}{\ampere\hour} against
\SI{1.2}{\ampere\hour} nominal. The mechanism is that the capacity C-rate enters
as a parameter rather than being learned: on LFP the first clustering layer
finds no clean capacity cluster and a second, stricter layer recovers it, with
the target rate supplied by configuration at both stages.

The supervised route exposes a design failure worth reporting. A random forest
trained on NMC and applied to LFP labels \emph{none} of the \num{16} capacity
discharges correctly, and every check-up consequently reports no state of
health. The cause is the C-rate: NMC capacity tests run
at C/2 and LFP at 1C, and a model trained on absolute pipeline labels has
learned ``capacity test'' to mean ``a full discharge at C/2''
(Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} isolates this under controlled folds).
Retraining the
same model on a scale-free description of segment shape --- full or partial,
charge or discharge --- and resolving that description against the target cell's
$C_{\mathrm{cap}}$ at inference recovers all \num{16}, reproducing the
unsupervised result exactly. On sodium-ion, both routes recover all \num{6}
check-ups.

\paragraph{Why a model trained at C/2 recognises a discharge at 1C.} The
result is initially counter-intuitive --- the model has never seen a 1C capacity
test --- so it is worth being explicit about what changes, because nothing about
the model, the features or the training data does. Only the question does.

In the absolute space the model is asked \emph{is this the capacity test?} That
question is rate-dependent: its ``yes'' examples all sit at a normalised current
of \num{0.5}, so an LFP discharge at \num{1.0} is outside the class it learned
and there is no route by which the model could know that this cell's capacity
rate is different. In the shape space it is asked \emph{what shape is this?} ---
a question with no rate in it. A full discharge is a full discharge whether it
takes one hour or two, and the training class reflects that: on our fleet its
members already span normalised currents from \num{0.05} to \num{1.0}. A 1C full
discharge is therefore an ordinary member of a class the model learned from NMC
and sodium-ion cells, not an extrapolation.

The rate then enters exactly once, after the model has finished, as arithmetic
rather than inference: is this segment's \emph{measured} current within
\SI{5}{\percent} of the rate this cell's configuration declares? LFP declares
1C and the segment measures 1C, so it resolves to a capacity test. Under the
controlled folds of Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} --- training on NMC and
sodium-ion, testing on LFP --- all \num{39} LFP capacity tests are predicted
\texttt{full\_discharge} and all \num{39} resolve correctly.

The chemistry-specific quantity is never learned, so it never has to transfer.

The sodium-ion result carries a caveat that must be stated with it: the
scale-free model tags \emph{two} capacity segments per check-up in four of the
six programmes. It resolves capacity by C-rate alone and does not test for a
full voltage swing, so a partial discharge at the capacity rate is accepted. The
downstream capacity calculation rejects the extras --- it requires a preceding
full charge --- so the reported capacity is unaffected, but label precision is
not \SI{100}{\percent}. Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} quantifies this and shows
the coulombic feature removes it.

\subsection{Check-up detection recall, and a fixed-threshold baseline}
\label{sec:res-recall}

Sections~\ref{sec:res-metadata} and~\ref{sec:res-loco} ask whether segments are
cut and labelled correctly. This section asks the question the method exists for:
given a cell's raw record, are the check-ups found at all?

\paragraph{Ground truth.} The cycler writes one file per test, and the procedure
name in each file's header states whether that test was a check-up. That
assignment is made once, at ingest, and is independent of everything downstream:
it says \emph{where the answer is}, while the method has to find the check-up
inside the signal. Using it as ground truth to score a method that never reads
it is therefore not circular. It is, however, weaker than a written schedule,
and we state the limitation rather than the stronger claim: it counts check-ups
\emph{present in the data}, so one that was scheduled but never ran, or ran but
was never uploaded, is invisible here. Ground truth is read from the raw layer
rather than from the segmented output, so that a programme the segmenter dropped
entirely cannot disappear from the denominator.

A check-up counts as \emph{detected} when the pipeline produced a capacity value
for it, and as \emph{complete} when it also yielded a pulse and both quasi-OCV
branches.

\paragraph{Baseline.} The foils locate the capacity test with a fixed absolute
current --- in BatteryLife's MICH script, a hard-coded threshold near
\SI{2.37}{\ampere}, used to \emph{delete} the RPT \cite{batterylife}. We
reproduce the structure of that approach rather than its constant: a single
absolute current, calibrated on one chemistry (NMC, whose capacity test is C/2
on a \SI{3.0}{\ampere\hour} cell, i.e.\ \SI{1.5}{\ampere}), applied unchanged to
the others. A segment is called a capacity test when it is a discharge within
\SI{5}{\percent} of that current lasting at least \SI{30}{\minute}. This is not
a defeat of prior work --- the threshold was written for a different purpose, on
a single source. It measures what a fixed constant costs when the chemistry
changes.

Table~\ref{tab:recall} gives the result. Across \num{210} check-ups on three
chemistries the method makes \textbf{no false detections at all}, and every
check-up it does not detect is a fragment: the four misses are between
\SI{0.4}{\percent} and \SI{3}{\percent} of the median check-up file size, and
are aborted runs or temperature-criterion stubs that share the check-up's
procedure name. Above a \SI{10}{\percent} size floor, recall is
\SI{100}{\percent} on all three chemistries.

The baseline behaves exactly as its design predicts. On the chemistry it was
calibrated for it is indistinguishable from the method (\SI{98.8}{\percent},
the same two fragmentary misses). On both unseen chemistries it finds
\emph{nothing} --- \num{0} of \num{41} LFP and \num{0} of \num{6} sodium-ion
check-ups --- because LFP's capacity test runs at 1C on a
\SI{1.2}{\ampere\hour} cell (\SI{1.2}{\ampere}) and sodium-ion's at C/2 on a
\SI{10.5}{\ampere\hour} cell (\SI{5.25}{\ampere}), and neither is within
\SI{5}{\percent} of \SI{1.5}{\ampere}. The constant does not transfer because a
current in amperes is not a property of the test; the C-rate is.

This is the paper's argument in its simplest form. The same quantity ---
``which discharge is the capacity test'' --- is a hard-coded constant in the
baseline and a declared parameter in the method, and that difference alone is
the gap between \SI{0}{\percent} and \SI{100}{\percent} on an unseen chemistry.

\subsection{What carries transfer: the features or the label space?}
\label{sec:res-ablation}

Section~\ref{sec:res-loco} showed that one supervised model transfers and
another does not. Two things differ between them, and this section separates
them: the model could fail because its \emph{features} are chemistry-bound, or
because its \emph{label space} is. We hold the features, the model and the folds
fixed and vary only the label space.

\paragraph{Two label spaces.} In the \emph{absolute} space the classes are the
pipeline's own: CAP, PUL, qOCV and the leftovers. ``CAP'' there means ``a full
discharge at this fleet's capacity C-rate'', so the rate is inside the class
definition and must be learned from the training chemistries. In the
\emph{shape} space the classes describe only what the segment is --- full
discharge, full charge, partial discharge, partial charge, pulse --- with no
rate in any class name; a prediction is resolved to CAP afterwards by testing
the segment's own measured C-rate against the held-out chemistry's declared
$C_{\mathrm{cap}}$. The chemistry-specific number is learned in the first case
and supplied as configuration in the second.

\paragraph{Result.} In the absolute space, held-out capacity recall is
\textbf{zero} on LFP and on sodium-ion, and no feature set changes it --- not
removing the voltage-range feature, not adding the coulombic duration, not
substituting one for the other. In the shape space it is \SI{100}{\percent} on
all three chemistries, for every feature set.

The reason is visible in the training distribution. With LFP held out, every
training capacity segment sits at a normalised current of exactly \num{0.500} ---
NMC and sodium-ion are both C/2 --- while LFP's is \num{1.000}. The model has
learned ``capacity test'' to mean ``a full discharge at C/2'', and at prediction
time the LFP segments fall outside that region on most of the current features
at once. It is broad covariate shift, not a deficiency of any single feature,
which is why no feature substitution repairs it. Held-out sodium-ion fails for
the same reason from the other side: its capacity current, \num{0.476}, sits
just below the \num{0.500}--\num{1.000} band the model saw.

\paragraph{Where the features do matter.} Not for recall, but for precision.
Resolving a full discharge to CAP by its C-rate alone accepts any discharge at
that rate, whether or not it swept the full cell. On sodium-ion the shipped
feature set predicts \num{12} capacity segments where there are \num{6}: the six
extras are partial C/2 discharges whose measured rate (\num{0.476}) is
\emph{identical} to the true capacity tests', but whose corrected SoC swing is
\num{0.229} against \num{0.951}. Adding the coulombic duration
$|I|\,\Delta t / Q_{\mathrm{nom}}$ --- \num{0.104} for the extras against
\num{0.985} for the true ones --- restores precision to \SI{100}{\percent}
without changing recall. It is a scale-free measure of how much charge the
segment actually moved, and it is what the rate test alone cannot see.

\paragraph{A hypothesis this replaced.} We had expected LFP's flat discharge
plateau to break the voltage-range feature, by compressing the voltage span that
distinguishes a full sweep from a partial one, and had intended the coulombic
feature as the remedy. That mechanism does not occur in this data.
\texttt{Voltage\_range} separates full from partial discharges on LFP with a ROC
AUC of \num{1.0} (medians \num{0.90} against \num{0.04}), because a full LFP
discharge still traverses both knees of the curve even though its middle is
flat, while the partial discharges this protocol contains are small SoC-adjust
steps. The failure would require a partial discharge that is large in charge but
small in voltage; the largest partial here moves \SI{40}{\percent} of nominal
capacity, and none is confusable. We report this as a negative result: the
coulombic feature earns its place in this method, but for the precision reason
above, not the one we assumed.

\subsection{Validation on independent laboratories}
\label{sec:res-external}

Every cell in Table~\ref{tab:cells} was measured at one institute on one cycler
family, so none of the preceding results speaks to portability between
laboratories. We therefore ran the method against reference performance tests
from two independent laboratories --- ISU-ILCC and UConn-ILCC --- ingested
through thin format adapters and given nothing but their published cell
parameters. \textbf{No label from either laboratory was used.}

\paragraph{What an adapted dataset can and cannot test.} The adapters
synthesise the columns the pipeline expects, and for ISU-ILCC the procedure
field is a single constant while the state field is a per-sweep tag the adapter
itself invents. The metadata ablation of Section~\ref{sec:res-metadata} is
therefore \emph{not} run on these data: one cannot meaningfully ablate protocol
columns one has written oneself, and reporting such a number would be
circular. What the external data does test is the question that matters most
here --- given a foreign laboratory's record and its datasheet scalars, are the
check-ups found?

\paragraph{Ground truth and detectors.} One programme is one reference
performance test, guaranteed by both adapters' construction, so the denominator
needs no labelling. Three detectors run over identical segments: the two
labelling routes of Section~\ref{sec:labelling} as the pipeline actually runs
them --- \emph{clustering} and the shipped \emph{classifier} --- and the
\emph{configured criterion}, a full sweep that is a discharge within
\SI{5}{\percent} of the laboratory's declared $C_{\mathrm{cap}}$.

The configured criterion is reported in two implementations. The first states
it directly as a rule. The second learns it: a model trained on the in-house
fleet only, predicting segment shape with no C-rate in any class, whose
predictions are resolved against the external laboratory's $C_{\mathrm{cap}}$
--- the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} with a laboratory boundary
in place of a chemistry boundary. These are \emph{not} independent evidence, and
we do not present them as two results: the model is largely recovering the same
full-versus-partial cut the rule states outright. The variant is included only
to show that the criterion survives being learned rather than written, so that
the transfer claim of Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} is not an artefact of
having hand-written a good rule.

Both routes are run on both laboratories. This matters: which routes a dataset
has happened to be processed with is an accident of its history, and comparing
one laboratory under one route against another laboratory under another would
confound the route with the laboratory.

Table~\ref{tab:external} gives the result. \textbf{The configured criterion
recovers all \num{177} external capacity tests}, on laboratories whose cells
differ from ours in capacity by a factor of eight (\SI{0.28}{\ampere\hour} for
ISU-ILCC against \SI{3.0}{\ampere\hour} for our NMC) and whose capacity C-rates
match none of ours. It holds whether the criterion is written or learned: the
learned variant was never shown a segment from either laboratory, and was given
each laboratory's declared $C_{\mathrm{cap}}$ and nothing else.

\paragraph{What the external data found in our own code.} The numbers in
Table~\ref{tab:external} are the corrected ones. On first contact with
UConn-ILCC the unsupervised route recovered \SI{9.7}{\percent} of its capacity
tests, and the cause was a defect in our implementation rather than anything
about the laboratory.

The confirmation stage, when more than one cluster passes its current and
direction masks, selected the \emph{smallest} of them. Size carries no
information about which cluster is the capacity test, and the rule is reachable
only when at least two clusters qualify --- which our own protocols never
produce. Across all ten in-house cells exactly one cluster ever qualifies, so
the choice was vacuous and the heuristic had never been exercised. UConn-ILCC's
protocol does produce two: a constant-current capacity test at exactly
$C_{\mathrm{cap}}$, and a CC-CV discharge whose CV tail drags its mean about
\SI{5}{\percent} low --- still inside the \SI{\pm 5}{\percent} band --- and
raises its current standard deviation from \num{0.0} to \num{0.06}. Taking the
smaller cluster selected the CC-CV contaminant, four segments, and discarded the
thirty-three that were the capacity population.

Selecting instead the qualifying cluster whose mean current is closest to
$C_{\mathrm{cap}}$ --- the rule the first clustering layer's own fallback
already used --- raises UConn-ILCC from \SI{9.7}{\percent} to
\SI{100}{\percent} and changes nothing in-house: re-running all ten in-house
cells gives identical capacity counts and bit-identical capacity exports, and a
controlled comparison on the sodium-ion cell differs in none of its \num{132}
segments.

We report this rather than quietly fixing it, because it is the clearest
argument for the experiment. A latent defect had sat in the labelling path
indefinitely and \emph{could not} be found on our own fleet: exposing it
required a protocol that discharges twice near the capacity rate, which ours
never does. The configured criterion was unaffected before and after, in both
its implementations, since neither consults cluster size. Cross-laboratory validation is usually justified
as a generalisation check; here it functioned as a test of the implementation.

After the correction the unsupervised route recovers all \num{93} UConn-ILCC
tests and \SI{89.3}{\percent} of the ISU-ILCC ones.

Recall is not the whole picture, and the last column is where the shipped
classifier separates from the configured criterion. Both recover every test,
but the classifier tags \num{3.07} segments per test on ISU-ILCC and \num{1.84}
on UConn-ILCC, against \num{1.00} and \num{1.09} for the criterion. This is the same over-tagging the in-house sodium-ion result showed
(Section~\ref{sec:res-loco}) and has the same remedy: resolving by rate alone
admits partial discharges at that rate, and a scale-free measure of swept charge
excludes them. The pattern reproduces on foreign data, at a laboratory boundary
rather than a chemistry one.

\paragraph{Scope of the claim.} The two laboratories do not exercise the whole
taxonomy, and it is worth being precise about which parts they test. ISU-ILCC's
reference test is four charge/discharge sweeps at two rates: it contains no
pulses and no quasi-OCV, and the pipeline correctly reports none. UConn-ILCC's
adds a pulse sequence, recovered in all \num{93} of its tests by both routes,
but likewise contains no low-rate sweep --- no segment anywhere in its records
falls in a C/20 band, and the longest non-rest segment is a C/3 discharge. So
this section establishes cross-laboratory portability for the \emph{capacity}
test on two sources and for the \emph{pulse} label on one, and it establishes
nothing at all for quasi-OCV, for want of a foreign protocol containing one.
That the pipeline reports zero quasi-OCV segments on both sources is the correct
answer rather than a miss, and it is worth noting that it produces no false
positives there either.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What is and is not claimed}

The method is not metadata-free, and describing it that way would be wrong. It
requires the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:inputs}. What it does not require is a
description of the experiment's structure: which step is which, in what order,
under what indexing. That is the distinction that governs portability, because
the two kinds of information have different availability. Voltage limits,
nominal capacity and target C-rates are known before the cell is connected, are
identical for every cell of a type, and are stable across laboratories. A
step-index map is authored per protocol, is invalidated when the protocol is
edited, and does not exist at all for a dataset one did not generate.

\subsection{Where portability comes from}

Three results in Section~\ref{sec:results} are the same result seen from
different angles, and it is worth stating once.

Everything that transfers shares one property: the chemistry-specific quantity
($C_{\mathrm{cap}}$) enters as a \emph{parameter at the point of use} rather
than being absorbed into something fitted. Everything that fails absorbed it ---
the fixed-current baseline into a constant
(Section~\ref{sec:res-recall}), the absolute-label classifier into learned
weights (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation}). The failures are not partial: both go
to \SI{0}{\percent} on an unseen chemistry, because a quantity that has been
absorbed is not merely inaccurate off-domain, it is silently wrong.

The controlled comparison in Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} is what makes this
more than an observation about two implementations. Holding the features, the
model and the folds fixed and varying only whether the C-rate is learned or
declared moves held-out capacity recall from \SI{0}{\percent} to
\SI{100}{\percent}. No feature set we tried recovers any of that gap. So
portability here is a property of what the method \emph{refuses to learn}, not
of how its inputs are engineered --- and it is bought with roughly five
datasheet scalars, which is the trade the introduction claims is worth making.

This also bounds the claim. Feature design is not irrelevant; it sets precision
once recall is secured. Resolving capacity by C-rate alone accepts partial
discharges at that rate, and it took a scale-free measure of swept charge to
reject them. Parameterisation decides whether the method finds the right
segments at all; features decide how cleanly.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cross-laboratory evidence covers capacity only.} The in-house
fleet is single-institute; Section~\ref{sec:res-external} adds two independent
laboratories, but their adapters give an independent ground truth for the
capacity test alone, not for the pulse and quasi-OCV labels. Both external
sources are also NMC-type, so laboratory and chemistry are not fully crossed.
\item \textbf{The signal-only segmenter was measured, not adopted.}
Section~\ref{sec:res-metadata} evaluates it as an ablation of the shipped
implementation. We report what it recovers, not a system built on it, and the
rest-structure cost noted there would have to be addressed first.
\item \textbf{Check-ups, not cycling.} The method is developed and evaluated on
check-up data. Nothing here establishes behaviour on cycling profiles.
\item \textbf{Small $n$ per chemistry} for LFP and sodium-ion as reported here
\todo{resolve with the larger fleet, see Section~\ref{sec:data}}.
\item \textbf{Two residual metadata uses} remain outside boundary detection: a
state-based filter drops a small class of cycler bookkeeping rows, and files are
grouped by test identifier. Neither describes protocol composition, and the
second has a single-group fallback, but both should be disclosed rather than
defended.
\end{itemize}
